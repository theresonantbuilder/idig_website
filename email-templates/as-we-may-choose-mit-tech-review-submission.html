As We May Choose

September 14, 2026  ·  J. Paul Duplantis

Recap — The Questions This Essay Raises

To prevent a future where we lose control over our own experiences, we must build communication technology that lets us discover who we are more than influence us on what to think.

In 1945, Vannevar Bush wrote an essay titled “As We May Think” that laid the groundwork for technology to tap into the storehouse of human knowledge. Bush believed the tools of his day focused too much on the physical and too little on the intellectual, and he imagined that trails of associative links could usher in a new age of communication — one that would extend the capabilities of human thought and action.

That manifesto inspired the technologists who gave rise to the web as we know it: Douglas Engelbart, Ted Nelson, Tim Berners-Lee, and others. But the very thing that connected us in new and revolutionary ways also began to influence us in ways Bush could not have anticipated. Those associative links, once envisioned as extensions of human thought, became increasingly optimized for corporate influence.

Engelbart's 1968 “Mother of All Demos” offers a glimpse of what might have been. His vision of computing wasn't about putting more information in front of an individual. It was about extending human capability through collaboration, shared knowledge, and new ways of interacting with information.

The future Bush predicted did give us remarkable new ways to access and navigate vast libraries of information. What he didn't predict was how the erosion of personal agency over that library could throttle the very potential he was hoping to unlock.

Looking backward isn't as sexy as looking forward, in a world filled with promises of frontier AI models curing cancer and automating the workforce. But we need to look both into the past and into the pathways that reside within ourselves if we're going to move forward without surrendering our agency to the providers of our experiences.

With the rise of generative AI into the fabric of our daily lives, how we may think could soon be replaced by something closer to how the machine thinks for us if we continue to cede our agency to LLMs and algorithms.

Conventional algorithms are largely designed to move us in a straight line toward a transaction with information, rather than a relationship with it. As powerful as generative AI can be, we still have to ask the better question to get a better answer. But how do we always know the better question to ask, or the better path to take?

We don't. So how do we reclaim agency over our experiences in a noisy world?

Perhaps we begin with something deceptively simple: before we can understand something, we must first encounter it. And what we encounter is never the whole of what exists — we encounter it through a particular position, a particular context, a particular set of relationships. The same piece of information can mean very different things depending on what surrounds it. A number without its units, a recommendation without knowing why it was made, a headline without its history — all of these can produce an interpretation that feels complete while remaining incomplete.

This is where the observer becomes important. The observer participates in the process of creating an interpretation. We don't simply receive the world; we encounter it, interpret it, connect it to what we already know, and decide what to do next. The experience changes us, and that changed position affects what we are capable of seeing next.

What we experience are potential positions within a structure of possible relationships. Each position opens pathways to others. When we observe, we collapse those possibilities into the outcome we choose. The structure does not determine where we go; it makes the pathways visible enough for us to choose.

And that's the part worth sitting with: these structures don't just mediate how we think. They mediate what becomes available for us to choose in the first place.

Physicists have a mathematical language — Hilbert space — for describing a system's full range of possible states before any single outcome is observed. Some researchers in a field called quantum cognition have borrowed pieces of that language to model human judgment, on the theory that a decision, like a quantum system, can hold multiple possibilities right up until the moment we commit to one.

I'm not asserting that human choice is literally a quantum process. But the image is a useful provocation: how much of a decision exists as a possibility before we observe ourselves making it? And if you'd had access to better context before making it, could you have chosen differently — for yourself, and for the people your choice touched?

Those possibilities exist within the context of what we observe. But many of the systems through which we observe the world intentionally limit that context. They optimize for engagement, conversion, retention, and revenue. The result usually isn't misinformation. It's something quieter: relevant information stays invisible because surfacing it doesn't serve the objective of the system delivering it. The context and meaning are already there, in what surrounds us — it's our lack of access to it that creates the friction.

It is the dimension and intention of thought that builders should be capturing as we build toward the future. The greatest frontier we can tackle may not be another external system at all. It may be the illumination of ourselves, which then shines onto others. That illumination comes from what we learn and what we share. When learning and sharing become better aligned with our own potential, communication becomes more than the transmission of information. It becomes a means of discovery.

So when Bush wrote about how we may think, he was solving a retrieval problem — how to manage a volume of knowledge that was already overwhelming the individual. He was remarkably prescient. But we've now largely solved retrieval. The question is what comes next. We've built tools capable of mining vast quantities of information. But we don't own the tools. And increasingly, we don't decide where or how we dig.

Breadcrumb Trails

Bush spent a good part of his essay describing how associative links could serve as trails to knowledge. But who is laying the breadcrumbs along those trails today?

Amazon is a good place to start. When you search for a product, you find what you want and often receive it within a day. The system is extraordinarily good at reducing the friction between desire and transaction. But look more closely at what happens between the search box and the purchase: the breadcrumbs are placed there, intentionally. The products presented to you, the order they appear in, the recommendations that follow, the “customers also bought” associations, even the language surrounding a listing — all of it is a pathway constructed by someone, or increasingly something, other than you.

A social feed works the same way, just with a different currency. You open the app to check on one thing and, an hour later, you're somewhere you never intended to go. Nobody forced you down that path one click at a time. But the sequence of what you saw, in what order, wasn't neutral — it was tuned to keep you moving, the same way Amazon's trail is tuned to keep you buying. The destination is attention instead of a transaction, but the mechanism — an invisible hand deciding which of the countless available connections gets shown to you next — is identical.

The remarkable achievement of the internet was connecting almost anything to almost anything else. The commercial achievement of the algorithm was deciding which of those connections were worth showing you. That distinction matters. Bush imagined associative trails as extensions of human thought. What we built, in practice, is a system in which the trails themselves became the product.

If I'm free to follow an association wherever my curiosity takes me, the trail extends my agency. If the trail is continually optimized to keep me moving toward someone else's outcome, my agency is being used to optimize someone else's objective. The breadcrumb hasn't disappeared. It has become invisible.

This isn't an argument against Amazon, or feeds, or recommendations, or commerce. These systems solve real problems — nobody wants to search through ten million products to find a pair of shoes. The question isn't whether something should help us navigate. The question is who gets to decide where the trail leads.

We should choose.

And that starts with building the alternative rather than just naming the problem.

What Building the Alternative Looks Like

For the past two years I've been building i-DIG, a small attempt at answering these questions in practice rather than just in argument. The premise is narrow on purpose: instead of sorting things by category or popularity, i-DIG maps them by the shape of what someone is actually hungry for in the moment — a feeling, a sense, a kind of story they need right now — and lets that shape, rather than a genre label or a ranking algorithm, become the trail.

The first place I've tested this is film discovery, where “what am I in the mood for” is a question genre labels answer badly. A trail called something like “the maverick against the system” surfaces a very different, more honest set of matches than a “top action movies” list ever could, because it's organized around the appetite rather than the metadata.

The part that matters more than the movies is the principle underneath it: the trail is visible and legible to the person following it, rather than a black box optimized against them. You can see the shape of the path you're on. That's the difference between a trail that extends your agency and one that quietly redirects it.

I'm not alone in circling this problem, even if most of the industry is approaching it from the opposite direction. The current wave of AI products — personalized feeds, shopping copilots, chat-based recommendation engines — is mostly building faster, more convincing versions of the same trail-laying Amazon already perfected: systems that infer what will move you and then move you there, just with a more articulate interface.

I believe a smaller but growing set of builders are pointing to the same underlying technology — embeddings, vector similarity, large language models — in the other direction: toward tools that show people the map instead of just walking them down one path on it.

Interfaces that let you see and steer the associative space you're in, rather than just receive its output. Open protocols for portable preference and identity data, so the trail you build belongs to you rather than to whichever platform you happened to be standing on when you built it.

None of this is solved yet, and I don't think any single project — mine included — gets to declare victory here. But the tools finally exist to make agency a design choice rather than an aspiration, and that's a genuinely different moment than the one Bush or Engelbart were building in.

The question they left unanswered was how we would navigate an ocean of information once we'd built our way past the problem of finding it. I don't think the answer is a better algorithm. I think it's a visible trail, and the right to choose which one to follow.

As we may choose.
